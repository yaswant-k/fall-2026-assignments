## Working title
**When explanation is not enough: Testing verification prompts in AI-assisted information decisions**

## Background and integration
Information systems increasingly do more than store and retrieve information: they help users decide what to believe, select, or act upon. Zhang and An (2025) illustrate how machine-learning systems can turn observational performance data into models that guide football decisions. Such models supply structured advice, but a numerical prediction does not by itself establish how a person should judge its reliability. Ibdah et al. (2021) show a complementary issue in digital consent: users may believe they understand a disclosure while failing an objective comprehension question. Guo et al. (2022) demonstrate that presentation density and organization shape search effort and satisfaction. Hou et al. (2026) move from information presentation to reasoning behavior, finding that critical-thinking scaffolds can reduce direct adoption of AI material in a collaborative learning context.

Taken together, these studies suggest that useful information, readable presentation, and critical appraisal are separate design obligations. An interface might present a technically accurate recommendation while failing to help a person recognize when to question it. A user might also trust a short, polished explanation without checking the underlying evidence. The proposed research tests an interface intervention aimed at this tension rather than assuming every explanation improves judgment.

## Research question
**Among undergraduate students completing AI-assisted information-evaluation tasks, does requiring an evidence-verification prompt in addition to a concise explanation reduce acceptance of inaccurate AI recommendations, compared with concise explanations alone or recommendations without explanations?**

The question is focused on one decision mechanism (verification prompts), researchable with controlled tasks, feasible within a course research-design project, narrow enough for clear outcomes yet sufficiently complex to support experimental analysis, and relevant to responsible AI adoption in information systems.

## Hypotheses
- **H1 (primary):** In tasks where AI advice is inaccurate, the explanation-plus-verification condition will have a lower rate of accepting the incorrect recommendation than the explanation-only condition.
- **H2 (secondary):** Explanation-plus-verification will yield higher final answer accuracy than advice-only, without substantially increasing erroneous rejection of accurate recommendations.
- **H3 (exploratory):** Prior AI use and self-reported trust may moderate the relationship between interface condition and reliance; no direction will be assumed without evidence.

## Thesis statement
**AI decision-support interfaces should combine concise evidence presentation with active verification opportunities because understanding a recommendation and appropriately relying on it are distinct behaviors; an experimental comparison can determine whether a verification step improves users' discrimination between sound and unsound advice.**

## Scope, definitions, and feasibility
The study will use simulated informational decisions suitable for adults without sensitive personal stakes. A task presents a question, relevant source evidence, and an AI-generated recommendation intentionally accurate or inaccurate according to a prechecked answer key. *Uncritical acceptance* means adopting an inaccurate AI recommendation when the task evidence supports the opposite answer. *Appropriate reliance* means accepting correct AI recommendations while rejecting incorrect ones, assessed separately rather than conflating all AI refusals with good decisions. The study addresses immediate task outcomes, not long-term trust or clinical/legal decisions.

The design is a planned experimental investigation, not a claim that data have already been collected. Part 3 specifies conditions, sample targets, measurements, analysis, risks, and ethics.
